# Keeping the headline honest when a few models are compared on the same folds

Researched 2026-11-23, against primary sources: the methodology papers on selection bias in cross-validation (Cawley and Talbot 2010, Varma and Simon 2006, Tibshirani and Tibshirani 2009, Boulesteix and Strobl 2009, Wainer and Cawley 2021), on the variance of cross-validation (Bengio and Grandvalet 2004, Nadeau and Bengio 1999, Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani 2023, Vanwinckelen and Blockeel 2012, Bouthillier et al. 2021), on tests across data sets (Demšar 2006, Benavoli et al. 2017), the neuroimaging and EEG decoding sources (Varoquaux et al. 2017, Varoquaux 2018, Combrisson and Jerbi 2015, the two MOABB benchmark papers, Roy et al. 2019), and the scikit-learn 1.9.1 and SciPy 1.18.0 documentation.

**What this feeds:** a protocol for comparing a few bigger models against the linear quadrant classifier, and a rule for when a gain counts as real. The split and the metric are fixed by `docs/adr/0005-one-quadrant-classifier-per-participant.md`: 32 participants, 40 trials each, one model per participant, stratified 5-fold by trial with one seed, balanced accuracy per window, averaged within a participant and then over participants. The reference scores a mean of 0.583 with a standard deviation of 0.053 across participants, and the bar for a new model is a gain of about 0.03.

**Confidence note:** nothing was trained or measured for this note. Every figure for *our* setting (the size of the optimism, what a test can detect, what a design costs) is arithmetic or a small simulation from the two numbers above and from the published formulas, and each is marked "our arithmetic". The quantity all of them depend on, the standard deviation across participants of the paired difference between a candidate and the reference, has not been measured. Most papers were read in full text; Varma and Simon, Boulesteix and Strobl, and the documentation pages were read through a fetch summary, not line by line. The list at the end says what could not be verified.

## Summary

1. **The unit of evidence is the participant, not the fold or the window.** The 32 participants are independent; folds of one participant share training trials and the 15 windows of a trial are near-copies. Every source that compares methods across subjects or data sets tests on one number per subject (Demšar, MOABB), and the sources on fold-level variance say fold-level tests are optimistic (Bengio and Grandvalet, Nadeau and Bengio, Varoquaux).
2. **With 2 to 5 candidates and a mean over 32 participants, the optimism from picking the best is small next to the 0.03 bar**: about 0.002 to 0.012 if no candidate is truly better (our arithmetic), because the selection happens on a 32-participant average and the candidates share most of their noise. The published horror figures (12 points of bias on null data) come from one data set of 40 samples and searches over many settings. The optimism grows with every extra recipe tried, counted or not, which is the ADR's "few knobs" guard.
3. **Nested cross-validation over trials is the textbook answer and costs the most data here**: inner models train on 24 trials, not 32. A nested loop **over participants** removes the same bias for free: each participant's reported score comes from the recipe that won on the other participants, using scores already computed.
4. **A held-out set is the cleanest claim and the weakest measurement.** Eight held-out trials per participant, or eight held-out participants, give a standard error two or more times the full design's. Useful as a one-shot confirmation, not as the headline.
5. **Repeated cross-validation removes no bias.** It averages out the luck of one partition, which sharpens the paired differences, and it costs only compute. It converges to a value specific to these 40 trials.
6. **The paired test for 32 differences: Wilcoxon signed-rank, exact, one-sided, each candidate against the reference, Holm-corrected, reported next to the mean difference and its interval.** A paired t-test is also defensible at n = 32. Neither a test nor a correction un-biases the winner's point estimate.
7. **Shortlist:** (A) a fixed candidate list, same folds, paired participant-level test against the reference, and the headline taken from the participant-nested estimate; (B) the same plus a one-shot lockbox of participants for the final confirmation.

## 1. What goes wrong, and how large it is

### The mechanism

Choosing the configuration that maximises a cross-validation score, and then reporting that score, reports a maximum over noisy estimates. Cawley and Talbot name the cause as the variance of the selection criterion on a finite sample and show the resulting bias "can be surprisingly large" and "of comparable magnitude" to the differences between learning algorithms ([Cawley and Talbot 2010](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html), abstract; sections 5.2 and 5.3 give the tables). Their section 5.3 is our case exactly: model selection done once on all the data, then the same data re-partitioned for evaluation. They find a consistently optimistic bias on all thirteen benchmarks "even when the number of hyper-parameters is small (in this case only two)", and conclude the protocol "should not be used in practice". Their fix: "model selection should be viewed as an integral part of the model fitting procedure, and should be conducted independently in each trial" (section 6).

scikit-learn's guide says the same in one line: parameters "can be tweaked until the estimator performs optimally", so "knowledge about the test set can 'leak' into the model" ([cross-validation guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html)), and its nested-CV example adds that the size of the effect "is primarily dependent on the size of the dataset and the stability of the model" ([nested CV example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_nested_cross_validation_iris.html)).

### Published sizes

| Source | Setting | Optimism of the selected score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Varma and Simon 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1397873/) | 40 samples, 6,000 features, no real signal, tuning one or two parameters over a grid | Mean CV error 37.8% (shrunken centroids) and 41.7% (SVM) against a true 50%. Nested CV gave 54.2%, slightly pessimistic. |
| [Boulesteix and Strobl 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2813849/) | 62 and 102 samples, labels permuted, best of 124 classifier variants | Median minimal error 31% and 41% against 50%. The choice of classification method was the largest single source. |
| [Tibshirani and Tibshirani 2009](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2904) | Simulations and microarray data | Bias is larger with no signal, and "only an issue when p ≫ N"; it varies by classifier, so "it can be misleading to compare the CV error rates when choosing between models" (section 6). |
| [scikit-learn nested CV example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_nested_cross_validation_iris.html) | Iris, 150 samples, a 2-parameter SVM grid, 30 trials | Mean difference 0.0076 in accuracy, non-nested above nested. |
| [Wainer and Cawley 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09446) | 115 data sets, 12 algorithms | The flat estimate is biased upward, but flat and nested CV pick algorithms "of similar quality" when there are "relatively few hyperparameters". The authors restrict this to choosing a model: it "cannot be applied by a scientist whose goal is to provide evidence that one classification algorithm is better than another" (section 4). |

Two readings matter for us. First, the bias is a property of how many alternatives were tried and how noisy each estimate is, so the large figures belong to single small data sets and wide searches. Second, Wainer and Cawley's reassurance covers *which* model to pick, not *what number to report* for it or whether it beats a baseline, which is our question.

### Our size (our arithmetic, not from a source)

Our selection is made on a mean over 32 independent participants, which is far less noisy than one 40-sample data set. Write `s_d` for the standard deviation across participants of the paired difference (candidate minus reference, per participant). The noise in a candidate's mean gain is `s_d / √32`. If none of `k` candidates is truly better, the expected gain of the best is that noise times the expected maximum of `k` standard normals (0.56, 0.85, 1.03, 1.16 for `k` = 2, 3, 4, 5, by simulation), less if the candidates' errors are correlated:

| `s_d` | best of 2 | best of 3 | best of 5 | chance the best shows ≥ 0.03 (k = 5) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.02 | 0.002 | 0.003 | 0.004 | < 0.0001 |
| 0.04 | 0.004 | 0.006 | 0.008 | 0.0001 |
| 0.06 | 0.006 | 0.009 | 0.012 | 0.012 |

`s_d` is not measured. Its ceiling is roughly the 0.053 spread of the scores themselves if the two models' errors were unrelated; paired models on the same folds usually share most of it. So for a declared handful of candidates the optimism is a third of the bar at worst and a tenth more plausibly. This holds only while the count is honest: every architecture, feature set, learning rate and epoch count looked at on these folds is a candidate. Twenty informal tries behave like `k` = 20 (expected maximum 1.87 standard errors).

The 0.053 itself is mostly measurement noise, not stable differences between people: 40 binary trials at 0.58 give a binomial standard deviation of 0.078 per participant at trial level, and window-level scoring averaged over two axes lands below that. Combrisson and Jerbi's table makes the same point from the other side: with 40 samples and two classes, a score needs 62.5% to pass p < 0.05 against chance ([Combrisson and Jerbi 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jneumeth.2015.01.010), Table 1), so no single participant's 0.58 means much. The claim lives at the group level. Varoquaux recommends exactly this for within-subject decoding: "conclusions should be drawn from the group, and not at the subject level, where the small sample size tends to compromise cross-validation" ([Varoquaux 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07581), section 3.3).

## 2. Nested cross-validation

**What it is.** An outer loop scores; inside each outer training set an inner loop does the choosing. The outer test data "has not been used to optimise the performance of the model in any way" ([Wainer and Cawley 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09446), section 1). What it scores is the *procedure* (fit plus selection), not one named model ([Cawley and Talbot 2010](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html), section 5.1).

**What the sources recommend.** Cawley and Talbot: required for an unbiased comparison. Varoquaux et al.: "Any parameter tuning should be performed in nested cross-validation, to limit optimistic biases", yet for non-sparse linear decoders "relying on the default value of the parameter often outperforms parameter tuning by nested cross-validation" ([Varoquaux et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05201), guidelines and concluding remarks). The MOABB benchmark tunes inside each fold with an inner 3-fold loop for its classical pipelines, and used the published settings without search for the deep networks because the search space was too large ([Chevallier et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15319), sections 2.3 and 5). Boulesteix and Strobl note nested CV handles parameter tuning but is awkward when the thing chosen is the classification method itself.

**Two ways to nest in our design.** ADR 0005 says a recipe is judged by its average over participants and "never tuned per participant", so the choice is shared, and the loop can sit on either axis.

- **Over trials, with a shared choice.** In each of the 5 outer folds, every candidate is cross-validated on each participant's 32 training trials (say 4 inner folds of 8), the candidate with the best inner mean over all participants is fitted on the 32 and scored on the 8 held out. Cost: inner models see 24 trials (360 windows), a quarter less than the 32 the headline models see, so the inner ranking is of smaller-data models, and bigger models lose more from that than the linear one. Runs: `k` × 5 × 4 × 32 = 640`k` inner fits plus 160 outer, about 20 full runs for `k` = 5. No data is set aside.
- **Over participants (cross-fitting).** Each candidate is run once on the stated split, giving a 32 × `k` table of per-participant scores. Split the participants into groups (for example 4 of 8, or leave one out); for each group, pick the candidate with the best mean on the *other* participants and record that candidate's score on this group. The mean of the recorded scores estimates how a recipe chosen this way does on a participant who had no say in choosing it. Cost: no extra fits and no data given up, because participants are independent and nothing is trained across them. This is Cawley and Talbot's rule (selection repeated inside every evaluation trial) applied on the axis where our selection happens. No source describes this exact arrangement for per-subject decoders; it is our construction from their principle.

**What it buys here.** It removes the selection optimism in expectation (the 0.002 to 0.012 above). It adds some variance, and when candidates are close different groups pick different winners, so the number belongs to "the best of these `k`", which is the honest description of what was done.

## 3. A held-out set

**What the sources recommend.** scikit-learn: "A test set should still be held out for final evaluation" ([cross-validation guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html)). Boulesteix and Strobl recommend untouched validation data when available and suggest it is practical only from roughly 150 samples. Varoquaux's table of confidence bounds for a binary accuracy (±15% at 30 samples, ±10% at 100, ±6% at 300, [Varoquaux 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07581), Table 1) is the argument against small held-out sets, and his example of a public challenge whose 30-subject and 28-subject test sets disagreed by errors "on the order of ±15%" is the demonstration (section 2.1). His closing advice for methods work is a different kind of hold-out: "The best way to compare approaches without loophole is to test them across several datasets" (section 3.3).

**Held-out trials (our arithmetic).** Setting aside 8 of each participant's 40 trials leaves 32 for choosing (4 folds of 8, fits on 24) and scores the winner once on 8. Trial-level noise per participant rises by √5 ≈ 2.2, to a binomial standard deviation near 0.17, so the 32-participant mean has a standard error around 0.02 to 0.03 against about 0.009 today: a 0.03 gain is one standard error. Under the fixed rating threshold the most lopsided participant has 6 trials in the smaller class (ADR 0005), so an 8-trial set can hold one or two of them and balanced accuracy becomes very coarse. It can be used once; a second look makes it a validation set.

**Held-out participants (our arithmetic).** Setting aside `m` participants costs no trials. The confirmation is a paired comparison on `m` differences, and what it can detect at 80% power (one-sided 5%) is a mean gain of 0.98 `s_d` at `m` = 8, 0.77 `s_d` at 12, 0.65 `s_d` at 16, against 0.45 `s_d` with all 32. So a lockbox of 8 confirms a 0.03 gain only if `s_d` is about 0.03 or less. The selection set shrinks too (24 participants), which raises the optimism it is meant to remove. Two limits specific to us: the reference's scores on all 32 participants are already known, so only candidates can be blind to the lockbox; and a held-out participant watched the same 40 videos, so it tests "a new person, these stimuli", which is also all the full design claims.

**What it buys.** A number no choice has touched, with no argument about how many candidates were really tried. At this size it buys that at two to three times the standard error.

## 4. Repeated cross-validation

**What the sources say.** Varoquaux et al. recommend many random splits with 20% left out, in blocks that respect the dependence structure, because "the larger the number of splits, the smaller the variance" ([Varoquaux et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05201), results and guidelines). scikit-learn's model-comparison example uses 10 × 10-fold ([statistical comparison example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_grid_search_stats.html)). Bouthillier et al. find that variance from data sampling, weight initialisation and hyperparameter choice all "impact markedly the results", and recommend that "as many sources of variation as possible should be randomized" and that a claimed improvement "account for variance" instead of comparing averages ([Bouthillier et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03098), abstract and section 1).

**What it does not do.**

- It does not remove selection bias: the same trials are reused in every repetition.
- Its average "converges to another value than any of the values one might really be interested in", a quantity tied to this one data set, so more repetitions give a tighter estimate of the wrong target; "repeated cross-validation should not be assumed to give much more precise estimates" ([Vanwinckelen and Blockeel 2012](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/186558), section 5).
- Scores from the repetitions are not independent samples. There is "no universal ... unbiased estimator of the variance of K-fold cross-validation", and naive estimators "grossly underestimate variance" ([Bengio and Grandvalet 2004](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v5/grandvalet04a.html), abstract). Standard CV intervals had miscoverage "two to three times larger than the nominal rate" ([Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.00673), section 1). A t-test over the 5 × R fold scores of one participant is therefore invalid without the Nadeau and Bengio correction, which inflates the variance by `1/J + n_test/n_train` in place of `1/J` ([Nadeau and Bengio 1999](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/1661-inference-for-the-generalization-error), section 3; formula as given in the [scikit-learn example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_grid_search_stats.html)). Even that correction is a heuristic built for i.i.d. samples, not for 15 correlated windows per trial.

**How it fits our design (our reading).** Use repetitions to get a better *score per participant*, then test across participants. Demšar states the principle: resampling within a data set "is used only to assess the performance score and not its variance. The sources of the variance are the differences in performance over (independent) data sets" ([Demšar 2006](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v7/demsar06a.html), section 3). Averaging each participant's score over R partitions removes partition luck from each paired difference, which shrinks `s_d` and makes the participant-level test more sensitive. How much it shrinks is unmeasured. The per-fold scores themselves stay out of the test: Demšar knows of no test that can use them, "since training data in multiple random samples overlaps" (section 3.2.3). A single partition is already valid for the participant-level test, since partition luck is independent across participants and sits inside `s_d`.

**Cost.** `k` × R full runs: 50 runs for 5 candidates at R = 10, under an hour for models as cheap as the linear one. No data. For a model with random initialisation, a fresh initialisation seed per repetition folds that source in at no extra cost. ADR 0005 states one fixed seed for the headline and the app's stored predictions, so repetitions would sit beside the stated split as a comparison tool, or the ADR would change.

## 5. Paired tests across participants

### Which test

The observations are 32 paired differences, one per participant, each a difference of balanced accuracies on the same folds.

- **Wilcoxon signed-rank.** Demšar's recommendation for two classifiers over multiple data sets: it "does not assume normal distributions" and outliers "have less effect on the Wilcoxon than on the t-test" ([Demšar 2006](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v7/demsar06a.html), abstract and section 3.1.3). It is the EEG benchmark convention: MOABB uses a one-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-subject scores for data sets with more than 20 subjects, and a permutation-based paired t-test below that ([Chevallier et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15319), section 2.4; [Jayaram and Barachant 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.06427), section III), and it is the test deep-learning EEG papers use most when they test at all, which only 19.9% do ([Roy et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05498), section 3.7.5).
- **Paired t-test.** Demšar's three objections are incommensurable scores across data sets, non-normal differences "unless the sample size is large enough (∼ 30 data sets)", and outliers (section 3.1.2). The first does not apply to us (same task, metric and trial count for every participant) and 32 sits at his threshold for the second. It tests the mean difference, which is what the headline is. When its assumptions hold the Wilcoxon test is somewhat less powerful; when they fail it can be more powerful (section 3.1.3).
- **Sign test.** Assumes least and is "much weaker than the Wilcoxon signed-ranks test" (section 3.1.4). Not worth it at 32.
- **Several candidates against one reference.** Compare each to the control only, and correct with Holm, which "is more powerful than the Bonferroni-Dunn's and makes no additional assumptions"; "we thus should not make pairwise comparisons when we in fact only test whether a newly proposed method is better than the existing ones" (section 3.2.2). Demšar's full procedure starts with a Friedman test; with one reference and 2 to 5 candidates the Holm-corrected paired tests are the part that answers our question.
- **Bayesian alternative.** Benavoli et al. argue for a posterior over the difference with a region of practical equivalence, giving three probabilities (better, equivalent, worse) and so a way to say "no real difference", which a p-value cannot ([Benavoli et al. 2017](https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-305.html), section 3). The useful import even without the Bayesian machinery is the pre-declared margin: our 0.03 is such a margin.

### What the assumptions need from 32 differences

- **Independence between participants.** Holds by design: separate people, separate models, nothing fitted across them. It would break if anything were tuned on the pooled data between the two arms.
- **One number per participant.** Not folds, not windows. The Wilcoxon test assumes the differences are "independent and identically distributed" ([SciPy 1.18 `wilcoxon`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.wilcoxon.html)); fold scores of one participant and windows of one trial are neither.
- **Symmetry (Wilcoxon).** The signed-rank test assumes the differences come from a symmetric distribution and "is miscalibrated if the distribution is not symmetric" ([Benavoli et al. 2017](https://jmlr.org/papers/v18/16-305.html), section 4.1). A candidate that helps a few participants a lot and the rest not at all gives a skewed set; a histogram of the 32 differences is the check.
- **Continuous, unrounded differences.** Rounding creates ties and "would decrease the power of the test" (Demšar, section 3.1.3). SciPy computes the exact null distribution for up to 50 differences when there are no ties or zeros, and warns that with ties the default falls back to a normal approximation that "may be inaccurate" (`method="auto"`, SciPy 1.18 docs). Window-level balanced accuracy on 600 windows is fine-grained enough that exact ties are unlikely.
- **The estimand.** Wilcoxon tests a shift in location, the t-test the mean. The headline is a mean, so report the mean paired difference with a 95% interval beside whichever p-value is used.

### What 32 differences can detect (our arithmetic)

Smallest true mean gain detected with 80% power by a paired t-test on 32 differences, as a multiple of `s_d`: 0.45 one-sided at 5%, 0.51 two-sided, 0.57 one-sided at the first Holm step for four candidates (α = 0.0125). So a true gain of 0.03 is detectable if `s_d` is at most 0.067, 0.059 and 0.053 respectively, which the 0.053 ceiling suggests is likely. The other direction matters as much: an *observed* mean gain passes a one-sided 5% test once it exceeds 0.29 `s_d`, which is 0.009 at `s_d` = 0.03. Significance alone would therefore certify gains well under the bar; a rule needs both the test and the margin. The Wilcoxon test's power relative to the t-test under normal differences is about 0.95 by the standard textbook result, which was not looked up for this note.

### What a test does not fix

A significant, Holm-corrected test says the candidate is better than the reference. It does not correct the *size* of the winner's gain, which is still a maximum over candidates. The number to publish comes from section 2 or 3.

## 6. Shortlist

Stated as options for the decision, with the one measurement each needs first: `s_d`, from one run of any candidate beside the reference.

**A. Paired, pre-declared, participant-nested (no data given up).**

1. Write down the candidates (at most five) and their settings before any is scored on the 32 participants' folds. Settings come from defaults or from the source that proposed the model, following Varoquaux et al.'s finding that defaults often beat tuning at this size. Anything adjusted after seeing scores counts as another candidate.
2. Run each candidate on the stated split (same folds as the reference); optionally also on R repeated partitions, averaging per participant.
3. Report every candidate, not only the best (Boulesteix and Strobl's first recommendation), each with its mean paired difference from the reference, a 95% interval, and a one-sided exact Wilcoxon signed-rank p-value, Holm-corrected over the candidates.
4. A gain counts as real when the Holm-corrected test passes at 5% **and** the interval's lower end is above zero **and** the participant-nested estimate of the winner's gain (section 2) is at least the 0.03 margin. The nested estimate, not the winner's raw mean, is the new headline.

Cost: `k` full runs (or `k` × R), no data. Weakness: it relies on the candidate count being honest, and on the scores of all 32 participants having been seen only through this procedure.

**B. A plus a participant lockbox.** Before step 1, set aside participants by a rule fixed in advance; do everything in A on the rest; then score the single chosen recipe and the reference on the lockbox once, and report that paired difference as the confirmation. This is the only option that survives an unknown number of informal tries. It is worth its cost only if `s_d` turns out small (about 0.03 or less for a lockbox of 8, 0.04 for 12); otherwise the confirmation cannot distinguish 0.03 from zero and A is the better use of 32 participants.

Not shortlisted: nested CV over trials (pays a quarter of each participant's training data to remove an optimism of about 0.01, and bigger models pay most); held-out trials (the least precise option, and class counts in an 8-trial set get too small); repeated CV alone (variance only).

## Not verified

- **`s_d`, the spread of paired differences across participants**, and the split-to-split spread of a participant's score. Nothing was run. Every figure marked "our arithmetic" depends on them, and the simulation assumes roughly normal differences.
- **That selection optimism is "about 0.01" in practice.** It is derived from the expected maximum of normal draws, not measured on these folds; a permutation or repeated-split check on the real score table would settle it.
- **The participant-nested (cross-fitted) estimate** is our application of Cawley and Talbot's principle. No source read describes it for one-model-per-subject decoding, and its variance at 32 participants was not simulated.
- **Bouckaert and Frank 2004** (replicability of the corrected resampled t-test, the source of the 10 × 10-fold recommendation) could not be fetched; it is cited only through the scikit-learn example and Benavoli et al.
- **Nadeau and Bengio's journal paper** (Machine Learning 52, 2003) was not read; the NIPS 1999 version was, and its formula was cross-checked against scikit-learn's and Benavoli et al.'s statement of it.
- **Varma and Simon, Boulesteix and Strobl, and the scikit-learn and SciPy pages** were read through a fetch summary. The Varma and Simon summary flagged a mismatch between the abstract and Table 1 on one tail-probability figure; that figure is not used here.
- **The Wilcoxon test's relative efficiency of about 0.95** and the expected-maximum constants are standard results stated from memory and simulation, not from a cited text.
- **Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, *The Elements of Statistical Learning*, section 7.10.2** (the wrong and right way to cross-validate) is the textbook statement of section 1 and was not re-read.
- **A sign-flip permutation test on the mean difference** (`scipy.stats.permutation_test`) would test the mean under a symmetry assumption only; its documentation was not read for this note.
- **Whether participants are exchangeable given shared stimuli.** All 32 watched the same 40 videos, so the test generalises to new people on these videos. No source was read on treating stimuli as a second random factor.
- **EEG emotion-recognition papers on DEAP specifically** were not surveyed for their comparison protocols; the EEG evidence here is from motor-imagery benchmarks, fMRI and MEG decoding methodology, and one deep-learning EEG review.
